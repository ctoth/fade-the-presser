Read the minutes. Fade the presser.

The Chair explains the decision on decision day; three weeks later the Committee’s minutes explain it again. This ledger pairs the two accounts verbatim, classifies each pair, and checks whether the bond market actually fades the presser. Every meeting, automatically.

Updated when minutes are published · quotes machine-verified against the source · quarrels welcome

Method

The unit of account is the claim pair: a condensed verbatim excerpt from the Chair’s press conference set beside a condensed verbatim excerpt from the minutes on the same point. Pairs are chosen for substance, not for disagreement, so an uneventful meeting reads as uneventful. Each pair is classified, not scored:

  • ContradictionThe two accounts cannot both be true — a fact, a count, a rationale, or whether something was said.
  • AttributionSame facts, but the Chair presents as the Committee’s view what the minutes give to a minority, to “some” or “a few”, or to the Chair alone — or the reverse.
  • EmphasisSame facts and attribution; materially different weight or framing.
  • ConsistentThe same substance in different words. Tone alone never changes a classification.

Minutes vocabulary is read the way Fed-watchers read it: “most” is consensus, “many” a strong majority, “several” a bloc, “some” and “various” well short of one. Each meeting is analysed in three independent runs; every quoted fragment in every run is checked verbatim against the Federal Reserve source text and a run with any unverifiable quote is discarded. The run shown is the median by number of contradictions, and the ledger says in how many runs a contradiction appeared at all.

The market test is the title taken literally. If the presser moves rates and the minutes move them back, the presser was faded. We use the 2-year Treasury yield (FRED series DGS2, daily close): the change on the meeting’s final day against the change on the day the minutes are released. Opposite signs count as a fade. It is a coarse, daily, one-instrument test — but it is a real number with a real unit, which the classification is not.
